# Los Angeles: food and nutrition insecurity

## The headline number

**1 in 4 (24%) of all L.A. County households experienced food insecurity in the past year** (data collected October 2025), statistically similar to the 25% recorded in the 2024 report — [USC Dornsife brief](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md).

## Five-year trend

17% (Dec 2021) → 24% (Jul 2022) → 30% (Jul 2023) → 25% (Oct 2024) → 24% (Oct 2025) — [USC Dornsife brief](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md).

## Who is affected

- Among low-income households (under 300% of the federal poverty line): **1 in 3 (35%)**, down from 41% in 2024 and 44% in July 2023 — [USC Dornsife brief](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md).
- **25% of food-insecure residents have incomes above 300% FPL**, a group the brief says "has grown over time" — [USC Dornsife brief](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md).
- 40% of all food-insecure households have at least one child; 60% of those affected are female; 57% are Hispanic/Latino; 53% are aged 18–40 — [USC Dornsife brief](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md).
- Nutrition insecurity (a related but distinct measure) dropped from 29% (Oct 2024) to 21% (Oct 2025) — [USC Dornsife brief](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md).

## Why, per the brief

The brief points to cost of living — **"the cost of living in L.A. is about 50% more than the national average"** — plus two 2025-specific shocks: January wildfires that "burned 57,500 acres and destroyed more than 18,000 structures," and immigration enforcement, where "fear of deportation has kept many families from public spaces, making it harder to buy groceries or access food assistance" — [USC Dornsife brief](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md).

## How this compares to the national rate

Nationally, 13.7% of U.S. households were food insecure in 2024, measured with the same USDA Household Food Security Module the LA brief uses — [USDA food security dataset](../sources/usda-food-security-dataset.md). LA County's 24% is nearly double that national figure. This comparison is valid because both use the identical USDA module, unlike the Seoul comparison below.

## A solution already operating here

[Everytable](../sources/everytable-program.md), an LA-founded public benefit corporation, sells meals with **"pricing scaled to the income level of the neighborhoods it serves,"** using per-zip-code income data, and has grown to more than 35 California locations. It is a direct, already-running example of a business model built around this exact affordability gap — see [Solutions in practice](solutions-in-practice.md).
