# Problem statement

## Region

**Los Angeles County.** Chosen over Seoul because LA's evidence is broader (all households, not one age group) and lets us state a specific, defensible countywide number — see [wiki/la-vs-south-korea.md](la-vs-south-korea.md) for why the two regions' headline rates can't be directly compared in the first place.

## Who

**All Los Angeles County households** — this problem statement is not scoped to one income band, age group, or demographic. The USC Dornsife brief's own finding is part of why: food insecurity in LA is not confined to low-income households anymore (see "Why it's stalled, not just large" below) — [la-food-insecurity.md](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md).

## How many

**24% of LA County households — about 814,000 households — experienced food insecurity in the year ending October 2025.** This is essentially unchanged from 25% in 2024, and the USC Dornsife brief states this directly: **"the rate among all county households remained essentially unchanged (25% in 2024 and 24% in 2025). This indicates that food insecurity has not improved for higher-income residents."** — [la-food-insecurity.md](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md).

This stagnation at the countywide level holds even though the low-income subgroup did improve: food insecurity among low-income households (under 300% of the federal poverty line) fell from 41% (2024) to 35% (2025) — [la-food-insecurity.md](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md). In other words, progress for the poorest households hasn't moved the countywide number, because **25% of all food-insecure residents now have incomes above 300% FPL**, a group the brief notes "has grown over time" — [la-food-insecurity.md](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md).

## Why it's stalled, not just large

The brief points to **cost of living** as the structural driver: **"the cost of living in L.A. is about 50% more than the national average."** — [la-food-insecurity.md](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md). That's consistent with a rate that holds steady across income bands: even households above the poverty line aren't earning enough to absorb LA's cost of living, while earning too much to qualify for assistance like SNAP/CalFresh.

The brief also names two 2025-specific shocks layered on top of that structural cost pressure: January wildfires that "burned 57,500 acres and destroyed more than 18,000 structures," and immigration enforcement, where "fear of deportation has kept many families from public spaces, making it harder to buy groceries or access food assistance" — [la-food-insecurity.md](../sources/la-food-insecurity.md).

## The statement

> In Los Angeles County, 24% of all households — about 814,000 households — were food insecure in the year ending October 2025, a rate essentially unchanged from 25% in 2024. Progress for the poorest households (down from 41% to 35% food insecure) has not moved the countywide number, because a growing share of the food-insecure — 25% — now earn above 300% of the federal poverty line. The primary driver is LA's cost of living, about 50% above the national average, compounded in 2025 by wildfire displacement and immigration-enforcement fear keeping families from groceries and food assistance.

## Known gap, not yet sourced

The team also wants to explore **SNAP/CalFresh enrollment or benefit gaps** as a driver — specifically, whether households earning too much for CalFresh but too little for LA's cost of living are falling into a coverage gap. **No file in `sources/` currently documents CalFresh/SNAP enrollment or benefit-cliff data**, so per this repo's rules that claim isn't in the problem statement above. This is flagged as follow-up research, not asserted as fact.
